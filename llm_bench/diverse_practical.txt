The NASA Systems Engineering Handbook is intended to provide guidance and information that will be useful to the NASA community. It provides a generic description of systems engineering as it should be applied throughout NASA. A goal of the handbook is to increase awareness and consistency across the Agency and advance the practice of systems engineering. From a process perspective, product verification and product validation may be similar in nature, but their objectives are fundamentally different. Verification of a product shows proof of compliance with requirements, showing that the product can meet each "shall" statement through test, analysis, inspection, demonstration, or a combination of these methods. Validation of a product shows that the product accomplishes the intended purpose in the intended environment and meets the expectations of customers and other stakeholders. Verification asks whether the product was built right; validation asks whether the right product was built.

When developing requirements, it is important to identify an approach for verifying the requirements. The requirements verification matrix defines how all requirements are verified. Only "shall" requirements should be included in these matrices. The matrix should identify each "shall" by unique identifier and be definitive about the source document from which the requirement is taken. A project may divide the matrix into multiple matrices, for example one for each requirements document, to delineate sources of requirements. The example matrix provides guidance for the minimum information that should be included.

The verification and validation plan is baselined after comments from preliminary design review are incorporated. In the annotated outline, the word "system" indicates the entire scope for which the plan is developed. This may be an entire spacecraft, an avionics system, or a card within the avionics system. The terms "end item," "subsystem," and "element" imply lower-level products that, when integrated together, produce the system. The general term "end item" is used to encompass activities regardless of whether the end item is hardware or software.

The purpose of the verification and validation plan is to identify activities that will establish compliance with requirements and establish that the system will meet customer expectations. The plan describes where the requirements for the system come from and how they are flowed down to subsystems and lower-level elements. It should indicate the method used to perform flowdown and bidirectional traceability of requirements, such as a spreadsheet, model, or other means, and it may point to the file, document, or spreadsheet that captures the actual requirements flowdown.

The program formulation phase establishes a cost-effective program demonstrably capable of meeting Agency and mission directorate goals and objectives. The formulation authorization document authorizes a program manager to initiate planning of a new program and to perform analyses required to formulate a sound program plan. The lead systems engineer provides technical planning and concept development during this phase. Planning includes identifying the major technical reviews that are needed and the associated entrance and exit criteria. Formulation involves one or more reviews followed by a key decision point.

The purpose of pre-Phase A concept studies is to produce a broad spectrum of ideas and alternatives for missions from which new programs and projects can be selected. The team determines feasibility of the desired system, develops mission concepts, drafts system-level requirements, assesses performance, cost, and schedule feasibility, and identifies potential technology needs and scope. Typical activities include identifying stakeholders, capturing expectations as needs, goals, and objectives, defining measures of effectiveness, developing a concept of operations, performing trade studies, identifying risks, and preparing preliminary plans.

During Phase A, the purpose is to develop a proposed mission or system architecture that is credible and responsive to program expectations, requirements, and constraints, including resources. Activities are performed to fully develop a baseline mission concept, begin or assume responsibility for needed technologies, and clarify expected reliance on human elements to achieve full system functionality. Systems engineers are heavily involved in developing and assessing the architecture and allocating requirements to architecture elements. The effort becomes more formal, and the emphasis shifts toward optimizing the concept design.

Marine weather forecasts involve human effort to predict environmental conditions for a given location and time through the use of forecast guidance from numerical models and current observations. The National Weather Service Marine Weather Services Program offers a broad range of marine forecast and warning products in both graphical and text formats. Mariners rely on these forecasts, tidal predictions, and guidance from weather, wave, and ocean circulation forecast models for safe and efficient navigation. The forecasts cover coastal waters, offshore waters, high seas areas, and specialized ocean regions.

NOAA's National Weather Service issues marine weather watches, advisories, and warnings for hazardous winds and sea conditions. It also issues watches, warnings, and advisories for other hazards to mariners, including dense fog, freezing spray, and special marine warnings for short-duration hazardous conditions such as high winds, large hail during thunderstorms, or waterspouts. A watch informs mariners that a significant or hazardous event is possible but not imminent. An advisory indicates that a hazardous event is coming or imminent but does not meet warning criteria.

The coastal and offshore waters forecast includes expected winds, seas, reduced visibility, and any precipitation. The synopsis gives a brief description of significant weather features and forecasts over the coastal and offshore waters through the forecast period. Emphasis is placed on the movement of low pressure, high pressure, fronts, and tropical systems. With respect to wind warnings, maximum sustained winds over a ten-minute period are used offshore, while sustained or frequent gusts to warning criteria are used in coastal areas within a twenty-four-hour period.

NAVTEX receivers must be programmed with proper NAVTEX station and subject identifiers in order to receive weather broadcasts. Weather warnings may be broadcast at any time. The United States Coast Guard may defer or shorten a scheduled weather forecast via NAVTEX to ensure delivery of more urgent navigational and safety warnings. Routine weather forecasts are normally broadcast four times per day, and repeats of Notices to Mariners may be broadcast in lieu of weather during regular time slots. Mariners are encouraged to receive both warnings and routine forecasts.

VOBRA, the High Frequency Voice Broadcast marine forecast transmitted by the United States Coast Guard, provides mariners with a general overview of large-scale environmental marine conditions for the next five days. The synopsis has a brief description of significant weather features and the forecast over offshore waters through the forecast period. Emphasis is placed on movement of low pressure, high pressure, fronts, and tropical systems. The forecast includes expected winds, seas, reduced visibility, and precipitation, with particular emphasis on winds over thirty-four knots and visibility below one nautical mile.

The National Weather Service glossary contains information on more than two thousand terms, phrases, and abbreviations used by the NWS. Many of these terms and abbreviations are used by forecasters to communicate with one another and have been in use for many years, including before many products were directly available to the public. The purpose of the glossary is to aid the general public in better understanding NWS products. Users may type in a term or browse alphabetically through the glossary entries.

The Forecast/Advisory from the National Hurricane Center is generally composed of several easily identified sections. The sections, when present, occur in a specified order. A typical advisory includes the storm center location, position accuracy, present movement, estimated minimum central pressure, eye diameter, maximum sustained winds with gusts, wind radii by quadrant, seas, extended outlook, requests for ship reports, and the time of the next advisory. The wind and sea radii are given in nautical miles and indicate the largest radii expected anywhere in each quadrant.

Cleaning and sanitizing a household after an emergency is important to help prevent the spread of illness and disease. When using sanitizing products, read and follow the safety instructions on the product. Never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner. Try not to breathe in product fumes, and if products are used indoors, open windows and doors to allow fresh air to enter. Wear protective equipment as needed, such as rubber or non-porous boots, gloves, and goggles, especially when cleaning after a hurricane or flood.

For food-contact surfaces that may have touched floodwater, wash with soap and hot, clean water, then rinse with clean water. Sanitize in a solution of one tablespoon of household chlorine bleach in one gallon of clean water and allow the surface to air dry. For non-food contact surfaces that do not soak up water, such as floors, sinks, certain toys, and tools, clean with soap and warm clean water, rinse, sanitize using a mixture of one cup of bleach to five gallons of water, and allow to air dry.

After a flood, throw out perishable food that has not been refrigerated or frozen properly because of power outages, food that may have contacted floodwater or stormwater, food with an unusual smell, color, or texture, food in packages that are not waterproof, and food in cardboard containers such as juice, milk, or baby formula boxes. Throw out containers with screw caps, snap lids, crimped caps, twist caps, flip tops, and snap tops if contaminated. Discard home-canned foods and cans that are bulging, open, damaged, discolored, moldy, foamy, or foul-smelling.

Clean and sanitize dishes, utensils, and food-contact surfaces that have been flooded in a four-step process. Wash items with hot, soapy water. Rinse with clean, safe water. Make a solution of one cup of unscented household chlorine bleach in five gallons of clean water and soak items for one minute, ensuring the solution completely covers them. For items that cannot be put into the solution, such as countertops, apply the solution with a cloth. Allow all items and surfaces to air dry before use.

When returning to a flooded home after a natural disaster, be aware that the house may be contaminated with mold or sewage. If flood or storm water has entered the home, dry it out as soon as possible to prevent mold. Never use a generator, pressure washer, or any gasoline-powered engine inside the home, basement, garage, or less than twenty feet from any window, door, or vent. Use safe water for drinking, cooking, personal hygiene, brushing teeth, washing dishes, preparing food, washing hands, making ice, and making baby formula.

Canning is a method of using heat and airtight containers to preserve food as nearly as possible in the condition in which it is served when freshly cooked. It is a desirable and economical method for preserving many foods so their use can be distributed over seasons and to places where they are not available fresh. USDA's "How Did We Can?" exhibit presents documents on canning techniques, consequences of improper canning, equipment evolution, canning through the world wars, and home canning practices after World War II.

Raw-packing is the practice of filling jars tightly with freshly prepared but unheated food. Such foods, especially fruit, will float in the jars. Entrapped air in and around the food may cause discoloration within two or three months of storage. Raw-packing is more suitable for vegetables processed in a pressure canner. Hot-packing is the practice of heating freshly prepared food to boiling, simmering it two to five minutes, and promptly filling jars loosely with the boiled food. The liquid added to foods should also be heated to boiling.

Hot-packing helps remove air from food tissues, shrinks food, helps keep food from floating in jars, increases vacuum in sealed jars, and improves shelf life. Preshrinking food permits filling more food into each jar. Hot-packing is the best way to remove air and is the preferred pack style for foods processed in a boiling-water canner. At first, the color of hot-packed foods may appear no better than that of raw-packed foods, but within a short storage period, both color and flavor of hot-packed foods will be superior.

The Federal Register provides legal notices, rules, proposed rules, and other documents from federal agencies. A typical notice identifies the agency, document citation, document number, document type, pages, publication date, and summary of the action. For Forest Service legal notices, the intended effect may be to inform interested members of the public which newspapers will publish notices of proposed actions and decisions. After an emergency, you may need to survive on your own for several days. Being prepared means having food, water, and other supplies to last for several days. A disaster supplies kit is a collection of basic items your household may need in an emergency. Store items in airtight plastic bags and place the entire disaster supplies kit in one or two easy-to-carry containers, such as plastic bins or a duffel bag. Consider unique household needs, including supplies for pets, older adults, and people with medical needs.

A basic emergency supply kit can include water, with one gallon per person per day for several days for drinking and sanitation; food, with at least a several-day supply of non-perishable food; a battery-powered or hand-crank radio and a NOAA Weather Radio; a flashlight; first aid kit; extra batteries; whistle to signal for help; dust mask to filter contaminated air; plastic sheeting, scissors, and duct tape to shelter in place; moist towelettes; garbage bags; plastic ties; wrench or pliers; manual can opener; local maps; and backup phone power.

Because you do not know where you will be when an emergency occurs, prepare supplies for home, work, and cars. At home, keep the kit in a designated place and have it ready in case you need to leave quickly. Make sure all family members know where the kit is kept. At work, be prepared to shelter for at least twenty-four hours with food, water, medicines, and comfortable walking shoes. In a vehicle, keep supplies available in case you are stranded.

FEMA's Ready Campaign makes publication files available to the public to download at no cost. Organizations may reproduce these materials to expand the reach of critical preparedness information. The publications include an emergency supply list, general preparedness brochures, hazard-specific guidance, and the Are You Ready? guide. The general steps emphasized across the materials are to be informed, make a plan, build a kit, and get involved. The materials are designed for households, communities, schools, organizations, and people with specialized preparedness needs.

OSHA has developed heat-related illness prevention materials to help employers identify potential sources of heat hazards and develop a plan to address them. An effective safety and health program includes management leadership, shown when owners, managers, and supervisors commit to controlling hazards, protecting workers, and continuously improving workplace safety and health. Employers should conduct routine workplace self-inspections to identify heat-related hazards, control the hazards that are found, and monitor and evaluate those controls to verify that they continue to be effective.

Supervisors and workers should receive training in a language and format they understand. Training topics include identifying and controlling heat hazards, understanding environmental risk factors, recognizing signs and symptoms of heat-related injuries and illnesses, understanding individual risk factors that may affect workers, administering first aid and CPR for heat-related illness, and knowing what to do in a heat-related emergency. Workers should know how to report symptoms that develop while working, and emergency instructions should be posted at the worksite and reviewed before hot work begins.

A heat illness prevention program is an ongoing system that plans for and ensures workplace heat safety. Employers conduct routine inspections to identify heat hazards, control identified hazards, and monitor controls for effectiveness. Workers should be trained regularly on heat illness risks, symptoms, response procedures, and prevention methods. A monitoring system should watch for signs and symptoms of heat illness. A buddy system can help supervisors observe workers. More robust programs may include medical evaluations and monitoring of core temperature, hydration, pulse, or blood pressure on the job.

Remember: water, rest, shade. Designate a shady or cool area for breaks and provide cool drinking water. Portable tents and shelters can be useful where natural shade is unavailable. Ensure drinking water is available and accessible, and encourage workers to drink about one cup every fifteen to twenty minutes. Workers should not drink more than forty-eight ounces per hour, because too much water or other fluids can cause a medical emergency when the concentration of salt in the blood becomes too low.

In an emergency where regular water service has been interrupted, such as a hurricane, flood, or water pipe breakage, local authorities may recommend using only bottled water, boiled water, or disinfected water until regular service is restored. Boiling or disinfection can kill most disease-causing microorganisms that may be present in water. However, boiling or disinfection will not destroy other contaminants, such as heavy metals, salts, and most other chemicals. Properly disinfected water should be used for drinking, cooking, prepared drinks, dishwashing, and brushing teeth.

If bottled water is not available, boil water. Boiling is sufficient to kill pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. If water is cloudy, let it settle and filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter before treatment. Bring water to a rolling boil for at least one minute. At higher elevations, boil for three minutes. After boiling, allow the water to cool and store it in clean containers with covers. If desired, pouring cooled water between clean containers can improve taste.

If water cannot be boiled, disinfect it with household bleach. Use only regular, unscented chlorine bleach products that are suitable for disinfection and sanitization as indicated on the label. Do not use scented bleach, color-safe bleach, or bleach with added cleaners. If the water is cloudy, let it settle and filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter. Store the settled and filtered water in clean containers with covers, add the proper bleach amount, stir, and let it stand before use.

Other emergency water disinfection methods may include common household iodine or water disinfection tablets. For iodine, use only the recommended tincture and follow dosage instructions, increasing the amount if water is cloudy or colored. Stir and let the water stand for at least thirty minutes before use. Commercial tablets should be used according to the manufacturer's instructions. These methods are for emergency treatment of water that may contain microorganisms; they do not remove chemical contamination, salt, or heavy metals, and local instructions should be followed.

The Library of Congress advises that digital files selected for preservation should be identified, organized, and copied. Save copies on at least two different storage media, such as a USB drive and an external hard drive, and keep those copies in separate physical locations. Migrate saved copies to a current storage medium about every five years. Institutions should consult technical guidelines for digitizing cultural heritage materials, which cover equipment, process, image performance metrics, quality management, metadata, and quality-control documentation, including routine review records.

Institutions choosing a digitization or reformatting service provider should consider preservation and conservation expertise. Stabilization of items is often necessary to acquire a good or complete image, and safe handling of collections is equally important. If original formats are to be saved, choose a service provider that also provides preservation and conservation services. If using a provider without preservation expertise, a local provider may reduce risks associated with shipping collections, especially overseas. Microfilming and digitization preserve intellectual content but do not preserve the original item itself.

FADGI is a collaborative effort started in 2007 by federal agencies to articulate common sustainable practices and guidelines for digitized and born-digital historical, archival, and cultural content. Its still-image guidelines represent shared best practices for digitizing materials such as textual content, maps, photographic prints, and negatives. The guidelines include technical imaging parameters, best practices, digital image conformance evaluation, and professional staff considerations. They also describe a star rating system for evaluating digital image quality across different original object types and project requirements.

For home users, if the primary objective of scanning personal collections is to share them more widely with family and friends, a carefully chosen home scanner is more than adequate. It can be helpful to review technical or trade publications comparing scanners to understand equipment limitations. The Library of Congress does not provide conservation treatment, digitization or reformatting services for the public, access to its equipment, project funding, appraisals, product recommendations, materials testing, preservation courses, or responses to vendors seeking to sell products.

For Golden Corn Cake, use three-fourths cup corn meal, one and one-fourth cups flour, one-fourth cup sugar, five teaspoons baking powder, one-half teaspoon salt, one cup milk, one egg, and one or two tablespoons melted butter. Mix and sift the dry ingredients. Add milk, the egg well beaten, and butter. Bake in a shallow buttered pan in a hot oven twenty minutes. For corn cake sweetened with molasses, mix and bake as Golden Corn Cake, adding molasses to the milk.

For White Corn Cake, cream one-fourth cup butter and add one-half cup sugar gradually. Add one and one-third cups milk, alternating with one and one-fourth cups white corn meal, one and one-fourth cups flour, four teaspoons baking powder, and one teaspoon salt, mixed and sifted. Beat the batter thoroughly, then add the whites of three eggs beaten stiff. Bake in a buttered cake pan thirty minutes. The method depends on light beaten whites and careful alternate additions of milk and dry ingredients.

For Pop-overs, use one cup flour, one-fourth teaspoon salt, seven-eighths cup milk, two eggs, and one-half teaspoon melted butter. Mix the salt and flour, then add the milk gradually in order to obtain a smooth batter. Add the egg, beaten until light, and the butter. Beat two minutes, using a Dover egg-beater. Turn into hissing hot buttered iron gem pans and bake thirty to thirty-five minutes in a hot oven. Small round iron gem pans are best for Pop-overs, especially for even shape.

For Maryland Biscuit, mix and sift one pint flour with one teaspoon salt. Work in one-third cup lard with the tips of the fingers and moisten to a stiff dough with milk and water in equal quantities. Toss on a slightly floured board and beat with a rolling-pin thirty minutes, continually folding over the dough. Roll one-third inch thick, shape with a round cutter two inches in diameter, prick with a fork, place on a buttered tin, and bake twenty minutes in a hot oven.

For Sour Milk Griddle-cakes, mix and sift two and one-half cups flour, one-half teaspoon salt, and one and one-fourth teaspoons soda. Add two cups sour milk and one egg well beaten. Drop by spoonfuls on a greased hot griddle and cook on one side. When puffed, full of bubbles, and cooked on the edges, turn and cook the other side. Serve with butter and maple syrup. The directions emphasize the visual signs that show when a griddle-cake is ready to turn.

To boil rice for soup in the East India fashion, pick and wash half a pound in warm water. Put it into a saucepan, pour two quarts of boiling water over it, and cover the pan closely. Set it in a warm place by the fire to cook gradually in the hot water. In an hour, pour off all the water. Set the pan on hot coals and stir up and toss the rice with a fork, so as to separate the grains and dry them without hardening.

For ox tail soup, have the butcher divide three ox tails at the joints. Rub them with salt and soak them in warm water while preparing vegetables. Put four onions peeled and quartered, a bunch of parsley, two sliced carrots, two sliced turnips, and two dozen peppercorns into a large pot or stew-pan. Add the tails and three quarts of water. Cover the pot, set it on hot coals by the fire, and simmer gently for about three hours, skimming carefully.

In choosing fresh fish, select only those that are thick and firm, with bright scales and stiff fins, gills of a lively red, and eyes full and prominent. In summer, as soon as fish are brought home, clean them and put them in ice until ready to cook. Even then, do not attempt to keep fresh fish until the next day. Mackerel cannot be cooked too soon, as they spoil more readily than any other fish. Oysters in the shell may be kept longer with careful washing and cool storage.
